ADAM-6052 Signal Map

Sixteen signals in and out over Ethernet. Every picture is clickable, and every panel has a plain-words explanation underneath.

What is this box?

The ADAM-6052 is a small box that sits between machinery and a computer network. On one side, wires from switches, sensors and lamps screw into it. On the other side, one network cable (Ethernet) plugs in.

That lets a computer far away see whether something is on or off, count how many times it pulsed, and switch things on or off. It has 8 pins for listening (inputs) and 8 pins for acting (outputs).

Nothing here needs hardware knowledge. The panels go in order, from the box itself outward.

1The module

LEDs show a sample state: inputs 0, 2 and 5 on; outputs 1 and 4 on.

In plain words

Inputs are where the module listens. A wire from a switch or sensor goes into an input terminal, and the module reports whether that signal is on or off.

Outputs are where the module acts. It can switch power to something, such as a lamp, a buzzer or a relay.

Isolated means the input wiring is separated from the module's own electronics by a barrier (listed as 2,000 V). A surge or a wiring mistake out in the field is then less likely to damage the module or your network.

The module also needs its own power supply, 10 to 30 volts DC, shown on the right. The little lights show which channels are on right now.

2Wiring an input

In plain words

An input has to tell the difference between on and off. What you connect decides how.

Dry contact means a plain switch, button or relay contact. It carries no voltage of its own. It only joins two wires, or does not. Here one side goes to ground and the other to the input. If the switch is open, the module reads 1. If it is closed to ground, it reads 0. That feels backwards, but closing the switch pulls the input down to 0 volts. Click the switch to see it.

Wet contact means the wire already has voltage on it, because it comes from something powered, like a sensor or another machine's signal wire. The module does not supply anything. It just measures the voltage. Between 10 and 30 volts it reads 1 (on). Between 0 and 3 volts it reads 0 (off).

The "?" zone is 3 to 10 volts. The module does not promise a correct reading there, so a healthy signal should never sit in it. A 12 volt signal is fine, but a flat battery sagging to 9 volts would land in the "?" zone and give unreliable readings. Drag the slider to see.

Quick test: is there voltage on the wire? Then it is wet. Is it just a switch that connects or disconnects? Then it is dry.

3One input, three jobs

Digital input Is it on?
Counter How many pulses so far?
Frequency How many per second?

Set per channel, from the module's web page. Counting tops out at 3 kHz.

In plain words

Each input pin can do one of three jobs. You choose per pin, on the module's own web page.

Digital input answers "is it on right now?" Good for a door switch or a warning light.

Counter keeps a running total of pulses, like the odometer in a car. It holds a 32-bit number, so it can reach about 4.29 billion before it wraps around to zero, and an overflow flag tells you when that happened.

Frequency answers "how many pulses per second?", like a speedometer instead of an odometer.

The limit is 3 kHz, which is 3,000 pulses per second. Pulses arriving faster than that get missed.

4Counting a spinning wheel

In plain words

Picture a gear on a spinning shaft. A sensor next to it gives one pulse every time a tooth goes past. Count the pulses and you know how fast the shaft turns.

The sum is two steps. RPM divided by 60 gives turns per second. Times the number of teeth gives pulses per second. At 2,200 RPM that is 36.7 turns a second, and with 60 teeth that is 2,200 pulses a second.

The input can follow 3,000 pulses a second. Faster than that, it drops pulses and the speed reads too low. The fix is a wheel with fewer teeth, or a sensor that gives one pulse per turn. Move the sliders until the bar turns red to see the limit.

Two ways to read it: in frequency mode the module hands you pulses per second directly. In counter mode you read the total twice, then divide the difference by the seconds between the reads. Either way the module does not know about RPM. The software reading it does the sum above.

The sensor must give a clean on-or-off pulse, such as a Hall or proximity sensor. A raw wobbly signal from a magnetic pickup needs converting first.

5Driving a load

1 A eachpulses to 5 kHz
In plain words

An output is the module switching power on and off for something else. That something is the load: a lamp, a buzzer, the coil of a relay.

Source type means that when an output turns on, the pin pushes voltage out. You connect the load between that pin and ground, and current flows through it. Press the button to see the path light up. The opposite design, called sink type, instead connects the load to ground and expects the positive voltage to come from elsewhere. Mixing the two up is a very common wiring mistake.

Each output is rated 10 to 35 volts DC and up to 1 amp. That suits a lamp, a buzzer or a relay. A big motor needs a relay or contactor in between. There is built-in protection if you draw too much current.

An output can also pulse, switching on and off up to 5,000 times a second, or delay its change by a set time.

Check the module's wiring diagram for exactly where the output power supply connects before powering anything.

6Talking to the world

also SNMPREST APIGCL logic rules
In plain words

The module has one network socket. Once it is on your network, different programs can talk to it, each in its own "language".

Modbus TCP is a very common language for industrial controllers. A PLC or SCADA screen asks things like "what is input 3?" and the module answers with a number.

MQTT works like a post office. The module posts a message ("input 3 changed to 1") to a server called a broker, and anything that subscribed receives it. TLS means the messages are encrypted.

SNMP is used by network-monitoring tools. REST API lets ordinary web programs talk to it with web requests.

The setup page: type the module's address into a web browser to choose what each pin does.

Peer-to-peer: one ADAM can pass its inputs straight to another ADAM's outputs, with no computer between them. A button here could light a lamp across the plant.

Logic rules (GCL): small if-this-then-that rules stored in the module, such as "if input 0 is on, switch output 1 on".

7Putting it together: brake to lamp

In plain words

The setup: the park brake switch puts 24 V on its wire when the brake is applied. That wire goes into input DI 1. A 24 V indicator lamp is connected between output DO 0 and ground.

What happens, in order:

  1. The brake is applied, so 24 V appears on the wire and DI 1 reads 1.
  2. Something turns DO 0 on (see below).
  3. DO 0 pushes 24 V to the lamp. Current flows through it to ground and it lights.
  4. The brake is released, DI 1 reads 0, DO 0 turns off, and the lamp goes out.

Who turns DO 0 on? Pick either. With a rule inside the module, the module itself says "if DI 1 is 1, then DO 0 is 1" and no computer is needed. With software commanding it, a computer reads DI 1 over Ethernet, decides, and sends a command back to switch DO 0.

The dashboard reads DI 1 over Ethernet at the same time. One input feeds both the screen and the lamp.

If the brake signal is a plain switch to ground instead (a dry contact), DI 1 reads 0 when the brake is applied, so the rule becomes "if DI 1 is 0". If you only want a lamp, the vehicle's own wiring could light it directly. The module is worth it when you also want the dashboard, or logic such as "lamp only if the engine is running".

In plain words

A mechanical button does not close cleanly. When you press it, the metal contacts spring against each other for a few thousandths of a second, so the signal flickers on, off, on, off before it settles. You feel one press. A fast input sees many.

Debouncing is a rule that turns that flicker into one clean press. It matters here because, with an input set as a counter, every flicker is counted as a pulse.

Lockout timer: on the first change, start a timer and ignore the pin. When the time is up, look once. Still pressed? Then register one press.

N matching reads: look at the pin every millisecond, and only accept a new state after N looks in a row agree.

The one rule: the wait has to be longer than the bounce. Try it. Leave the bounce at 8 ms and drag the wait down to 3 ms. The filter gives up too early and registers an extra press. Click New bounce a few times: some bounces get away with it and some do not, which is exactly why a short wait cannot be trusted. For the N-reads method, N times 1 ms must beat the bounce, because a 1 ms look can miss a quick flicker between looks.

Sensors with no moving contacts, such as proximity or Hall sensors, do not bounce. I could not confirm whether the ADAM-6052 has a built-in input filter, so check its manual before wiring a plain button to a counter input.
