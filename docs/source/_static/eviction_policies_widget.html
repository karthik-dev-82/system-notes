Worth knowing, beyond what's animated here
  • Click "Run LRU-vs-FIFO Divergence Demo" -- it plays the sequence A, B, C, D, A, E. Both caches fill identically with A/B/C/D. Re-accessing A does nothing visible under FIFO, but under LRU it moves A back to the "recently used" end. When E arrives and something has to go, LRU evicts B (the actual least recently touched); FIFO evicts A anyway, because FIFO's rule is purely "whoever arrived first leaves first," and access never changes that -- the single most common thing people get backwards about these two policies.
  • LFU protects by frequency, not recency -- access a key many times and it becomes very hard to evict even if it's not accessed again for a while, unlike LRU where one long gap is enough to make it the next victim. This widget's LFU breaks ties (equal access counts) by least-recently-used among the tied keys, a standard, explicit tie-break choice -- "LFU" alone doesn't fully specify behavior without one.
  • TTL is the odd one out: it doesn't care about capacity or access pattern at all until an entry's own clock runs out. Click "Advance Clock +5" a few times with nothing else happening and watch entries disappear purely from time passing. Real caches often combine TTL with a capacity-based policy as a backstop (this widget does too -- once TTL's cache is full and nothing has expired yet, it falls back to evicting the oldest-inserted entry, the same rule as FIFO).
  • All four are genuinely independent caches here, not four views of one cache -- that's what makes the side-by-side comparison meaningful: identical input, real independent state machines, different real answers.
